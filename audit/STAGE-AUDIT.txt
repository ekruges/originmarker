AUDIT: STAGE INFERENCE FROM SNP ARRAY HETEROZYGOSITY
Affymetrix Axiom UK Biobank, 825,657 markers, GRCh37. Preimplantation embryo material.
Figure: stage_audit.png

Bottom line up front. Claim 1 is correct in outline but wrong in its arithmetic, and the
copy-number ladder in claim 3 cannot be generated by the mechanism claim 1 proposes. Claim 2
is not a valid estimator, and the single most damaging finding in this audit is that it is
NOT MERELY IMPRECISE BUT SILENTLY BIASED BY ANCESTRY, which converts a European-anchored
constant into wrong stage calls on non-European samples. Claim 4 is storytelling and the
literature contradicts the specific mechanism. Claim 5 has the right architecture but a
false premise about first polar bodies. Claim 6 is unjustified and also not binding.

Q1. Claim 1, and is claim 2 a valid estimator?

Claim 1, the qualitative part, is right and is not controversial. A heterozygous locus in a
single cell has one molecule per allele before amplification, first-cycle priming failure is
unrecoverable, and the marker reads homozygous. Keep that.

The quantitative part does not survive. Your own ladder refutes the copy-number mechanism as
stated. If each template molecule fails independently with probability f, then dropout is
d = f^n for n starting molecules. Calibrate f on the single-cell rung (d=0.199, n=2) and you
get f=0.446, which predicts d = 5.5e-6 for a 15-template trophectoderm biopsy against your
measured 0.050 - four orders of magnitude off - and exactly 0 for bulk against your measured
0.013. Fitting a two-parameter version, d = e0 + (1-e0)*f^n with a floor e0 for
copy-number-independent failure, gives e0=0.0315 and f=0.479 with residual RMS 0.041; it
still cannot reproduce bulk at 0.013 and it necessarily assigns identical dropout to the two
n=2 rungs, which is the claim-4 problem restated.

What this means: the 0.013 and 0.050 rungs are NOT sampling dropout. Losing every one of 10^6
copies is impossible, exactly as you say, so whatever produces 0.013 in bulk gDNA is
genotyping error, cluster-boundary miscalls and real homozygosity - not template loss. Your
ladder is a mixture of two different physical processes reported in one column with one name.

Claim 2 is not a valid estimator. It is a difference of two quantities, only one of which is
dropout. Bias computed exactly on the forward model (all in dropout units, at true d=0.199):

  confound                          bias in d_hat
  2nd cousins (F=0.0156)                 +0.012
  1st cousins (F=0.0625)                 +0.050
  uncle-niece (F=0.125)                  +0.100
  5% of genome LOH or UPD                +0.040
  20% of genome LOH                      +0.160
  whole-chromosome-14 UPD                +0.023
  anchor 5% too high                     +0.040

Consanguinity at first-cousin level alone moves the estimate by a quarter of its value. These
biases are additive with each other and with ancestry (Q3), and none of them is detectable
from h.

The estimator is also internally contradicted by your own drop-in measurement, and this is
the cleanest single demonstration that it is not an estimator of dropout (figure panel a).
Drop-in adds spurious heterozygous calls at rate i over homozygous markers, so the honest
forward model is h = H(1-d) + (1-H)i. At your measured i = 0.039 to 0.0525, 1 - h/H returns
-0.180 to -0.247 for bulk gDNA. Negative dropout is not a value the parameter can take. Note
also what drop-in does to the single-cell rungs: at i=0.0525 roughly 27% of the heterozygous
calls in a blastomere are spurious. Both of your measured numbers cannot be true at once
under the current model. Under the no-drop-in model the blastomere rung predicts h = 0.1163,
which is your observed lowest-diploid figure of 0.116 to three decimals - so the ladder was
almost certainly derived under i=0, and the drop-in measurement was never propagated.

Two further checks against measurements already made in this project, both of which make the
problem worse rather than better.

Your drop-in figure is low. Prior work in this project measured drop-in on 7 real 1C WGA arrays
(donor B maternal pronuclei) at 0.0569-0.0939, median 0.0854 - roughly 1.9x the 0.039-0.0525 you
cite. Substituting the higher measured value, 1 - h/H returns -0.410 for bulk gDNA at i=0.0854
and -0.452 at 0.0939. Whichever drop-in number is right, the estimator is further from
self-consistency than panel a shows, not closer.

The bulk rung is quantitatively accounted for by genotyping error on this exact platform. The
same prior work measured 3.31% marker disagreement between technical replicates of ONE bulk gDNA
sample, 98.4% of it heterozygote-to-homozygote, implying a per-array het-loss rate near 0.016.
Your bulk "dropout" of 0.013 sits just inside that noise floor. So the claim-1 argument is
confirmed from the other direction: template loss is impossible in bulk, and the number you have
assigned to dropout there is fully explained by the array's own replicate error.

A better within-array estimator exists, and you already have the data for it. Two independent
amplifications of the same genome (your 113 same-genome array pairs): among markers called
heterozygous in at least one replicate, the discordant fraction is phi = 2d/(1+d), invert to
d = phi/(2-phi). Simulation confirms recovery at 1.00-1.01x of truth across d = 0.05 to 0.308
(figure panel d). It needs no population anchor and is therefore immune to F, LOH, UPD and
ancestry, because those change which markers are heterozygous but not the probability that a
heterozygous one survives amplification twice.

Two caveats you must document with it, the second of which I found by checking against this
project's own prior measurements.

First, correlated dropout biases it low, exactly proportionally. If a fraction rho of failures
is shared between replicates (hard-to-amplify locus, GC content, secondary structure), recovery
falls to 0.90x at rho=0.1, 0.75x at rho=0.25, 0.50x at rho=0.5. So the replicate estimator is a
lower bound on dropout, and the amount by which it underestimates is the amount of
locus-intrinsic failure. That is a documented uncertainty rather than a hidden bias.

Second, it has a floor set by the platform's own genotyping error, and that floor is not
negligible on this array. This project previously measured that technical replicates of the SAME
sperm-donor bulk gDNA disagree at 3.31% of markers, 98.4% of it heterozygote-to-homozygote. Feed
a bulk replicate pair to the discordance estimator and it returns roughly 0.10, which is
obviously not dropout in material with 10^6 template copies. So the estimator measures
"any discordance that loses a heterozygote," of which dropout is one component and cluster-
boundary calling error is another. Subtract a platform floor estimated from bulk replicate pairs
before reporting the remainder as dropout, and never apply it to bulk or ES-line material where
the floor dominates the signal. It is the right estimator for single cells, where dropout is
large relative to that floor, and the wrong one for the two rungs where it is not.

For single arrays with no replicate, the fallback is a per-marker likelihood using the parental
or population expectation per marker rather than one genome-wide constant, which at least lets
F and LOH be modelled as their own parameters instead of being absorbed into d.


Q2. The chromatin explanation. Storytelling - and the literature tests it directly.

Drop it. Piyamongkol et al. 2003 (Mol Hum Reprod 9:411-420, PMID 12802048, not retracted)
varied reaction conditions across >3000 single-cell amplifications specifically to identify
what drives ADO. Their finding: ADO was affected by amplicon size, DNA degradation, freezing
and thawing, PCR programme, and the number of cells co-amplified. Factors with little or no
effect included local DNA sequence, denaturing temperature, and - directly against your
claim - CELL TYPE. A mechanism you attribute to the intrinsic chromatin state of one cell type
versus another is the mechanism that study looked for and did not find.

The alternatives you listed are better supported than chromatin, and the strongest evidence
comes from a same-lab comparison that removes the between-laboratory confound you worried
about. Verlinsky/Rechitsky 1998 (J Assist Reprod Genet, PMC3454764, not retracted) measured
ADO in one lab with one assay across three cell types: single blastomeres 33.3% (delta-F508)
and 22.8% (beta-globin), single fibroblasts 7.1% and 7.7%, first polar bodies 5.9% and 9.6%.
Blastomeres show 3.0-4.7x the ADO of fibroblasts under identical chemistry, and both are
single cells with two template molecules. So a real single-cell-type effect exists and is
larger than your ~1.5x - but the same paper's polar bodies, with ONE genome, show the LOWEST
ADO of the three. Template copy number therefore does not order these data, and neither does
cell-cycle phase in the direction you proposed. Something about blastomeres specifically -
lysis behaviour, cytoplasmic carryover, biopsy damage, degradation state - dominates.

Suggested wording: "Single ES cells and cleavage-stage blastomeres both begin from two
template molecules yet show systematically different dropout. We do not know why. Reported
candidates that survive experimental test include DNA degradation state, amplicon size,
freeze-thaw and lysis chemistry (Piyamongkol 2003); cell type per se did not affect ADO in
that study, and we therefore do not attribute the difference to chromatin state. The two
figures may also not be directly comparable if they derive from different sample-preparation
pipelines. This parameter is empirical, not mechanistic."


Q3. Is 0.168 the right anchor? It is the wrong KIND of parameter, and it is not stable.

Two separate problems.

First, the number is not reproducible as a genome-wide per-marker heterozygosity for a
common-SNP array. On 60,000 biallelic 1000 Genomes chr20 SNPs, mean expected heterozygosity
in EUR is 0.323 for markers with EUR MAF>0.05 and 0.435 for MAF>0.20 - both far above 0.168.
Reaching 0.168 requires roughly half the panel to be rare content (EUR MAF<0.01), which is
plausible for a UK Biobank Axiom array with heavy rare/exome coverage, but it means the anchor
is a property of the panel's MAF spectrum, and any marker-QC change that alters which markers
pass alters the anchor. Documenting 0.168 as a platform constant without stating the marker
filter it was computed under is a reproducibility hazard.

Second, and this is the one to fix first: it varies with ancestry enough to change the stage
call. Using panel-composition-matched ratios from 1000 Genomes (mean expected het relative to
EUR): SAS 0.993, AMR 1.004, AFR 0.929, EAS 0.806. With a EUR-derived anchor, the dropout
estimate is biased by +0.005 (SAS), +0.057 (AFR) and +0.155 (EAS) for a sample whose true
dropout is 0.199 (figure panel b). The EAS ratio near 0.80 is robust: it holds at 0.802,
0.793, 0.855 and 0.841 across four independent ascertainment schemes, so it is not an artifact
of my panel model.

The consequence is not a mildly wrong parameter, it is a wrong stage (figure panel c). An
EAS-ancestry BULK gDNA sample returns d_hat = 0.205 and is classified as a single ES cell. An
EAS trophectoderm biopsy also classifies as single ESC. An AFR bulk sample classifies as a
trophectoderm biopsy. Every downstream call parameterised by stage is then wrong, and nothing
in the output indicates a problem, because the value returned is a perfectly plausible dropout
rate. This is the failure mode I would put in the release notes above all others.

On your proposed fix: estimating the anchor per-run from the most bulk-like array is better
than a constant, but it is circular - "most bulk-like" is judged by high h, which is the
quantity you are trying to calibrate, so a non-EUR run with no true bulk sample will elect a
low-dropout single cell as its anchor and compress the whole ladder. Two sound options
instead. Use the sample's own genotypes to estimate its expected heterozygosity from
population-frequency-free quantities (per-marker allele frequencies estimated across your own
cohort, or an ancestry projection onto 1000 Genomes principal components, then the matched
expected het). Or make the anchor unnecessary by using the replicate-discordance estimator
from Q1, which is the cleaner answer where a same-genome pair exists.


Q4. Is the haploid boundary safe at 0.105? Direction-dependent, and the premise is wrong.

The boundary placement itself is roughly defensible on sampling grounds, but only because you
have 825,657 markers. The premise underneath it is not.

The premise error: "a polar body carries one genome and is homozygous by construction, so its
heterozygous calls are all error" is false for FIRST polar bodies. A PB1 contains a dyad -
two sister chromatids of one homolog - and distal to every crossover the two sisters carry
different haplotypes. Those regions are genuinely heterozygous. Ottolini et al. 2015
(Nat Genet, doi 10.1038/ng.3306, not retracted; PMC4770575) mapped this directly in 23
complete human meioses and report a mean of 41.6 +- 11 maternal crossovers per meiosis, with
PB1 explicitly showing heterozygous regions where PB2 and the oocyte show haplotype
transitions. Simulating that measured crossover rate over autosomal physical lengths with one
obligate crossover per chromosome, the genuinely heterozygous fraction of a PB1 genome is
0.44 (5-95%: 0.34-0.55), giving expected h = 0.074 (5-95%: 0.058-0.092) with no dropout at all
(figure panel f).

So your stated haploid band of 0.002-0.10 is not one population, it is two: PB2, pronuclei and
sperm carry a single chromatid and have true het = 0, so their h is drop-in only, which your
own measurement puts at 0.039-0.0525; while PB1 sits near 0.06-0.09 for genuine biological
reasons. Any module that treats a PB1's heterozygous calls as pure error will mis-parameterise
it, and worse, the density of "error" will be highest exactly where crossovers are, which is
non-random with respect to position.

Failure in each direction, computed at N = 825,657:
- Haploid rising toward the boundary: PB2 with drop-in at 0.0525 is at h=0.0525, safely below.
  PB1 mean 0.074 is below, but the upper tail reaches 0.092, and a PB1 with drop-in on top can
  cross 0.105. P(PB1 model exceeding 0.105) is 0.003 at zero dropout - small but not zero, and
  my simulation excludes drop-in, so the real rate is higher.
- Diploid falling toward the boundary: a blastomere at d=0.308 sits at h=0.1163, only
  0.011 above the boundary. Add first-cousin consanguinity (F=0.0625) and it falls to 0.1090,
  which is 0.0007 above the line - effectively on it. Push dropout to 0.40 and h = 0.1008,
  below the boundary, and the sample is called haploid. So a heavily dropped-out or
  consanguineous blastomere is the realistic failure, not an exotic one.
- Triploid: falls the other way, h stays high, so it will not be called haploid - but it will
  be called low-dropout diploid, which is also wrong.

On your BAF suggestion: partly right, and my first attempt at testing it was wrong in an
instructive way. The FRACTION of markers in the intermediate BAF band is arithmetically almost
the same statistic as h (simulated mid-band fraction 0.0445 for a haploid with i=0.045, 0.1558
for a blastomere at d=0.308) so it adds nothing independent for the haploid-versus-dropped-
diploid discrimination. What IS independent is band POSITION: a triploid puts its
heterozygous markers at BAF 1/3 and 2/3 (simulated: 82% of mid-band markers within 0.08 of
1/3 or 2/3, mean |BAF-0.5| = 0.167 versus 0.049 for haploid and diploid). So BAF band position
should be required to exclude triploidy, and it does that job well. For the haploid/diploid
call itself the genuinely independent signal is total intensity - a haploid carries half the
DNA mass - but LRR is badly degraded by WGA amplification bias, so how much resolution
survives in your material is an empirical question I cannot settle from here.

Recommended second signal, in order of what the data supports: require BAF band position at
0.5 (excludes triploid), and for PB1 specifically expect heterozygosity in contiguous
tracts consistent with crossover structure rather than scattered uniformly. Scattered het is
drop-in; tract-structured het is real. That is a within-array test and it also gives you a
PB1-versus-PB2 discriminator you do not currently have.


Q5. Marker floors. Yes, there is a principled derivation, and it says 100/200 are wrong.

Derive the floor from the likelihood, not from a table. For distinguishing two stages with
expected heterozygosities h0 and h1, the expected log-likelihood ratio per informative marker
is the Bernoulli KL divergence, so the markers needed for posterior odds R (flat prior) is
N = ln(R) / KL(h0 || h1). At R = 99 (99:1 odds):

  bulk vs TE biopsy                32,235 markers
  TE biopsy vs single ESC           1,794
  single ESC vs blastomere          2,940
  PB1 vs blastomere                   468

Against a stated floor of 100-200 (figure panel e). At N=200 the bulk-versus-TE distinction is
separated by 0.17 sigma, an error rate near 0.47 - indistinguishable from a coin flip. Your
floor is 10-100x too low for the classification it is being asked to support.

Two things follow. First, the justification you have ("dropout is lower so fewer markers are
needed") is backwards for stage discrimination: lower dropout means adjacent stages are
CLOSER in h, so bulk-versus-TE is by far the hardest pair and needs the MOST markers, not the
fewest. Second, and more usefully: the floor is not the binding constraint. You have 825,657
markers, which exceeds every requirement above, so stage classification is never actually
marker-limited on a whole-array sample. The floors are doing a different job (guarding
downstream per-locus calls on sparse informative subsets), and they should be documented as
that with their own derivation, not as a stage-inference parameter.

If you want the floor to also control the precision of the dropout estimate, state it that
way: at N=200 the 95% CI on d_hat is +-0.28, which is wider than the entire ladder. At
N=10,000 it is +-0.04. Whole-array, +-0.005.


Q6. Material not in your list. This is where I would expect silent failures.

The module has no mechanism to detect that a sample is not one of its five categories, and
every unlisted type maps onto some point of the h axis and gets a stage.

  cumulus cells - somatic, diploid, maternal genotype but NOT the embryo's. h will look
  bulk-like or TE-like and the stage call will be roughly right, while the sample identity is
  wrong in a way that matters far more than its dropout parameter. Contamination of an embryo
  sample with maternal cumulus is the classic PGT failure and h cannot see it. Needs a
  distinct check (genotype concordance with the mother, plus BAF for mixture).

  multi-cell blastomere clumps - intermediate template count, so h lands between rungs. The
  module will assign whichever rung is nearest and understate the uncertainty. Benign relative
  to the others.

  cell fragments and anucleate material - arbitrary, very low template. h collapses toward the
  drop-in floor, which will read as haploid. A fragment classified as a pronucleus is a
  plausible and undetected failure.

  failed amplification - the dangerous one. Near-total dropout drives h toward 0, so a failed
  reaction is classified as HAPLOID rather than as failed, and gets a nonsensical parameter set
  applied instead of being rejected. This is the same direction as claim 5's stated failure but
  arriving from the other side, and the boundary cannot separate them. A call-rate and
  intensity gate must run BEFORE the haploid classifier, not after it.

  mosaic and aneuploid embryos - present in essentially any embryo cohort. Whole-chromosome
  and segmental changes shift h locally; chromosome-level UPD or LOH biases d_hat upward
  (whole chr14 UPD alone: +0.023) and the module reads it as a later, dropout-ier stage.

General principle: h is a one-dimensional statistic being asked to identify a category with at
least three independent dimensions (ploidy, template count, sample integrity). One number
cannot separate three axes, and every unlisted material type is an instance of that.


Wording to ship

Documentation and release notes currently state claims 1, 3 and 4 as fact. Suggested
replacements:

Claim 1: "Allele dropout arises because a heterozygous locus in a single cell is represented
by one molecule per allele before amplification; early priming failure is unrecoverable and
the marker reads homozygous. The rate is not a pure function of template copy number - the
values we use for bulk and multi-cell material are dominated by genotyping error rather than
template loss, and an independent-per-molecule sampling model does not reproduce the observed
ladder."

Claim 2: "We estimate dropout from heterozygosity shortfall against a platform anchor. This
is a correlate, not a measurement: consanguinity, copy-neutral LOH, UPD and ancestry
differences from the anchor population all depress heterozygosity and are absorbed into the
estimate. Where a same-genome replicate array exists, prefer the replicate-discordance
estimator, which requires no anchor; it underestimates dropout in proportion to the fraction
of locus-intrinsic (shared) failures."

Claim 3: "These are the empirical dropout values we apply per stage, measured on this material.
They are calibration constants, not predictions of a copy-number model, and the bulk and
trophectoderm values include genotyping error that is not template loss."

Claim 4: as drafted under Q2 above.

Claim 5: "Haploid material is classified before dropout is estimated. Note that first polar
bodies are NOT homozygous by construction: distal to each crossover the two sister chromatids
carry different haplotypes, so roughly 44% of a PB1 genome is genuinely heterozygous
(expected h approximately 0.074). Only PB2, pronuclei and sperm have true heterozygosity of
zero. The 0.105 boundary is a sampling-safe cut for our marker count but is not safe against
heavily dropped-out or consanguineous diploids, which can fall below it."

Claim 6: "Marker floors are set for downstream per-locus calling, not for stage inference;
stage inference on a whole array is not marker-limited. The floor required to distinguish
adjacent stages at 99:1 odds is 1,800-32,000 informative markers depending on the pair."


Priority order for fixes

1. Add a pre-classification QC gate (call rate, intensity) so failed amplifications cannot be
   classified as haploid.
2. Stop using a fixed EUR-derived anchor. Ancestry-match the anchor or switch to the
   replicate-discordance estimator. This is the one currently producing confidently wrong
   stage calls on real samples.
3. Propagate drop-in into the forward model, using this project's measured 0.0854 median rather
   than 0.039-0.0525; the current estimator is inconsistent with either value.
4. Correct the PB1 premise and add BAF band-position as a required second signal.
5. Rewrite claims 1, 3, 4 as above; re-derive the marker floors for the job they actually do.

Limits of this audit
Ancestry ratios are computed from 60,000 chr20 1000 Genomes biallelic SNPs under a modelled
panel MAF composition, not from the Axiom UK Biobank manifest. Direction and approximate
magnitude are robust across four ascertainment schemes; exact bias values would need the real
manifest intersected with your marker QC. The PB1 heterozygosity fraction is a crossover
simulation calibrated to a published mean rate (41.6 +- 11), using physical rather than genetic
map positions and assuming one obligate crossover per chromosome; it is an estimate of scale,
not a measurement on your samples. The correlated-dropout parameter rho is unmeasured in your
data - measuring it across your 113 replicate pairs would convert the main caveat on the
recommended estimator into a number. The two cross-check figures I bring in from elsewhere in this project (drop-in 0.0569-0.0939 on
7 donor-B 1C WGA arrays; 3.31% replicate discordance on one bulk gDNA sample) come from prior
sessions' measurements on GSE148488, not from re-measurement here - confirm they are the same
marker QC and platform build as your module's inputs before quoting them in the docs.
I could not obtain the full text of Piyamongkol 2003
(publisher blocks automated access); the cell-type finding is quoted from the PubMed abstract,
and the effect sizes behind it should be read from the paper before it is cited in your docs.
