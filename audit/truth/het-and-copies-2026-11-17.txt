Heterozygosity and intensity together: can they tell 0, 1, 2 and 3 copies apart
================================================================================
Written 17 November 2026. Measurement only: no tool code was written, built or released for it.
Open arrays only, every array checked against audit/local/truth/units.tsv before it was read.
Raw tables are untracked under audit/local/het-copies-2026-11-17/. Cells are numbered; the numbers
map to arrays only in a private file that stays on the measurement box.

THE OWNER'S IDEA, which this file tests
---------------------------------------
"By combining how haploid samples have very little heterozygous SNPs (the 0.5 BAF calls for
haploids are usually just background) maybe you can use both of this information to differentiate
between 0, 1 and 2 copies."

WHY IT WOULD MATTER
-------------------
The intensity the tool draws is relative: it is normalised to the sample's own level. A cell whose
every chromosome held the same number of copies reads flat, so the copy axis only means "copies"
while the rest of the cell is two. Heterozygosity has no such weakness. A region truly at one copy
cannot be heterozygous, so its heterozygous calls are background. Together the two should give an
absolute anchor, which the intensity alone cannot.

THE RULE TESTED, written before the numbers below
-------------------------------------------------
For each 2.5 Mb bin of each autosome, measure three things and nothing else:
  H  heterozygosity: the share of CALLED markers in the bin that read heterozygous. With both
     parents loaded it is read on opposite-homozygote markers only (father AA and mother BB, or the
     reverse), where two copies must be heterozygous; with one parent, on that parent's homozygous
     markers; with none, on every called marker.
  C  call rate: the share of all OFFERED markers in the bin that were called at all. A lost region
     stops calling.
  I  intensity: the median of the same intensity column the gain study reads, minus the median over
     the cell's own published-normal bins (and, where stated, minus the cohort's median offset for
     that same bin, which takes out the probe-density and GC bias shared by every array).
Then: group every bin by the copy number the publication states for its chromosome, and ask how
often each quantity, and the PAIR H x I, places a bin on the right side of each class boundary.
The claim under test, in one line: H separates one copy from two where I cannot, and I separates
nought and three where H cannot, so the pair settles all four classes.

Also tested, because they decide whether the idea survives contact with a real cell:
  D  allele drop-out per cell, measured on the chromosomes the publication calls normal, where
     every opposite-homozygote marker MUST read heterozygous at two copies. Drop-out is the share
     that reads homozygous instead. If a per-cell background rate taken from a cell's own normal
     chromosomes is enough to separate one copy from two in the WORST cells, the idea holds; if it
     is only enough on average, it does not.
  P  whole-genome ploidy, the one case the relative intensity cannot do at all: published pronuclei,
     which carry one parental genome and so sit at one copy throughout, against bulk and cell-line
     DNA at two copies throughout.
  X  the confusion: a region at two copies that has lost heterozygosity for another reason reads low
     on H and normal on I, while a true one-copy region reads low on both. Measured as: of the bins
     a publication calls normal, how many read H at or below the median H of the SAME cell's
     published one-copy bins, and does I then tell them apart.

WHAT WAS READ
-------------
292 arrays listed open with a file on disk. 85 of them carry at least one published whole-chromosome
copy state, giving 84,313 bins:
       0 copies     1,902 bins     8 cells    42 chromosomes
       1 copy       6,664 bins    27 cells   129 chromosomes
       2 copies    72,101 bins    77 cells  1,442 chromosomes
       3+ copies    3,646 bins    30 cells    83 chromosomes
Published states come from the Turocy 2026 and Zuccaro 2020 per-chromosome karyotypes. Nought copies
is a stated nullisomy whose own chromosome is named in the published phrase; a nullisomy named for
another chromosome in the same phrase, and the one array called "nullisomic" with no chromosome
named, are not used. The Jerabek 2026 and lab arrays carry no per-chromosome state and so appear
only in the ploidy and drop-out parts.

LIMITS OF THE ANSWER KEY, stated before the tables
  - Only whole-chromosome states are binned. Every segmental row in the open truth table has no
    start or end, so a segmental loss cannot be placed on the genome. Segmental GAINS do have
    coordinates in the open regions table and are included.
  - The four setups are the same cells with the parents withheld, as the gain study does it, because
    the open set has no cell whose only loaded parent is the father.
  - A chromosome carrying more than one published statement is dropped.
  - The pair figures come from a straight line fitted through the two quantities on the same bins it
    is then scored on. Read them as a ceiling on what the pair could do, not as a tool's accuracy.

WHAT SEPARATES
--------------
1. ONE COPY FROM TWO is the boundary the idea was meant for, and heterozygosity does settle it, but
   only with both parents. On opposite-homozygote markers, two copies read 0.667 heterozygous and
   one copy reads 0.083. That is 0.95 separation from heterozygosity alone, against 0.71 from the
   intensity. The intensity adds nothing on top: the pair is also 0.95. The owner's quantity is the
   one doing the work, and it is doing it better than the axis the tool draws today.
2. ONE COPY FROM TWO WITHOUT PARENTS is not settled by either quantity alone, and the pair is a real
   gain: 0.70 from heterozygosity, 0.77 from the intensity, 0.86 from the two together. This is the
   one place in the whole study where combining them beats both parts, which is the owner's claim,
   confirmed, for exactly the setup where the tool is weakest.
3. NOUGHT COPIES is settled, but by the CALL RATE, not by heterozygosity. A nullisomic bin calls
   0.438 of its offered markers against 0.852 on a normal bin, and its intensity sits 1.9 below the
   cell's own level. Heterozygosity does the opposite of what the idea expects there: see below.
4. THREE COPIES FROM TWO is settled by the intensity alone (0.95 with no parent, 0.84 with both) and
   heterozygosity does not help (0.62 to 0.69 across the four setups). Adding it slightly HURTS the
   pair with both parents loaded, 0.79 against 0.84 for the intensity on its own.
5. A REBUILT PARENT loses nothing. Read on the rebuilt father's homozygous markers, one copy against
   two is 0.71, against 0.66 for the measured father. Nought against one and two are within 0.01.

WHAT DOES NOT SEPARATE, and the reason
--------------------------------------
NOUGHT COPIES READS HETEROZYGOUS, NOT HOMOZYGOUS. This is the finding that breaks the simple form of
the idea. A nullisomic bin has no template, so the array reads background in both channels, the B
allele fraction lands near 0.5, and the caller emits AB. Measured: 0.665 heterozygous of called
markers at nought copies, against 0.151 at two copies and 0.105 at one copy. With both parents the
effect is total: heterozygosity separates nought copies from two at 0.52, a coin flip.

So heterozygosity is NOT monotone in copy number. Its order is
     nought (0.67, all background)  >  two (0.67 with parents, 0.15 without)  >  three  >  one
and a rule that reads "low heterozygosity means fewer copies" will call a nullisomy normal, or
better than normal. The owner's own parenthesis, that the 0.5 calls are background, is the whole
reason: at one copy the background is diluted by real homozygous calls, at nought copies it is all
there is. The quantity that tells nought from everything else is the call rate, which collapses by
half, and it has to be read first.

THE SECOND CLASS BOUNDARY THE PAIR CANNOT SETTLE is two against three. With both parents, three
copies read 0.604 heterozygous against 0.667 at two, a separation of 0.62; the extra copy is
heterozygous too, so there is nothing for heterozygosity to see. Only the intensity sees a gain.

ALLELE DROP-OUT, the thing that was expected to break it
--------------------------------------------------------
Measured on each cell's published-normal chromosomes, where an opposite-homozygote marker must read
heterozygous. 28 cells with both parents loaded (23 blastomeres, 4 trophectoderm, 1 zygote):

  drop-out   median 0.324   10th to 90th percentile 0.203 to 0.509   lowest 0.099   highest 0.568

A third of the markers that must be heterozygous read homozygous, and in the worst cell more than
half do. The per-cell background is therefore large and it varies three-fold across cells, so a
fixed threshold is useless; the question is whether each cell's own background is enough.

THE ANSWER, plainly: a per-cell background measured from the cell's own normal chromosomes is enough
in the typical cell and is NOT enough in the worst cells. Above a drop-out of about 0.30 the
heterozygosity reading holds up well (0.87 to 0.99 for eleven of the fourteen cells), but in the two
worst cells it falls to 0.65 and 0.85, and the pair only recovers them to 0.93 and 0.89. In cell 118
the whole separation comes from the intensity, not from heterozygosity. So the pair is more robust
than heterozygosity alone, and neither is clean in a cell that has lost half its heterozygous calls.
There is no drop-out level at which the measurement simply stops working, and no level at which it
is safe either; it degrades, and the cell's own background is the only thing that says where it sits.

WHOLE-GENOME PLOIDY, the case the relative intensity cannot do
-------------------------------------------------------------
15 published pronuclei, each carrying one parental genome and so at one copy throughout, against 34
bulk-DNA and cell-line arrays at two copies throughout.

  group                       n   het of called            call rate      per-chromosome
                                                                          intensity spread
  pronucleus, one copy       16   0.092 [0.074 0.291]      0.841          0.510 [0.333 1.948]
  diploid control            34   0.175 [0.144 0.187]      0.967          0.163 [0.137 0.315]

The intensity says nothing about the ploidy. Of the twelve published pronuclei whose call rate is at
least 0.70, nine have no chromosome deeper than -0.41 against their own genome median, where a true
one-copy chromosome inside a diploid cell sits 0.44 below and a nullisomic one 1.9 below. A haploid
genome reads as a normal genome, exactly as predicted, because there is nothing left to normalise
against. The other three each have one chromosome at -1.76 to -1.85, which is the nullisomy level:
the relative intensity can still see a chromosome LOST INSIDE a haploid cell, it just cannot see
that the baseline it is measuring against is one copy rather than two. The per-chromosome spread
does separate the two groups at 0.96, but in the wrong direction and for the wrong reason: the
haploids are the WIDER group (median spread 0.510 against 0.163), so that figure reads amplification
noise, not ploidy, and a rule built on it would call every noisy diploid cell haploid.

Heterozygosity does say it. On the twelve pronuclei whose call rate is at least 0.70, het of called
runs 0.061 to 0.122. Bulk DNA and cell lines run 0.141 to 0.345. Separation 1.00, no overlap at all.
Against single diploid CELLS, which carry drop-out of their own, the separation is 0.98 with one
overlapping pair out of 48 (haploid up to 0.122, single diploid cells from 0.118).

  YES: heterozygosity alone puts a haploid cell in the right class where the relative intensity
  cannot. It is the clearest result in this file.

The three pronuclei it fails on are the three with the lowest call rate (0.54 to 0.60), and they
fail upward: their het reads 0.25, 0.33 and 0.44, far ABOVE the diploid controls. That is the same
background that makes a nullisomic bin read heterozygous. The call rate has to gate the reading.

THE CONFUSION: two copies with no heterozygosity against one copy
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Of the bins a publication calls normal, how many read heterozygosity at or below the median of the
SAME cell's published one-copy bins, and does the intensity then tell them apart.

  both parents, 14 cells with both a one-copy and a two-copy chromosome
    two-copy bins reading at or below their own cell's one-copy level:  419 of 8,641  (4.8%)
    worst cell 19.8%, best cell 0.6%
    intensity separates those confusable two-copy bins from true one-copy bins:  0.56
    call rate separates them:  0.67

  no parent, 26 cells
    two-copy bins reading at or below their own cell's one-copy level:  11,156 of 19,617  (56.9%)
    nine of the 26 cells are above 80%
    intensity separates them:  0.79
    call rate separates them:  0.50

Two different answers. With both parents, the confusion is rare, 4.8% of real two-copy bins, but
when it happens the pair does NOT resolve it: the intensity separates those bins from true one-copy
bins at 0.56, a coin flip. Their intensity sits at -0.08 against -0.30 for true one-copy bins, with
a 10-to-90 spread of -1.16 to +0.42, so the deep tail of the confusable bins is indistinguishable.
With no parent the confusion is the normal case, 56.9%, and the intensity is the only thing that
helps, at 0.79. In other words: with parents the pair is accurate but cannot explain its own
mistakes; without parents the heterozygosity reading is nearly uninformative per bin and the
intensity carries it.

WHAT THE TOOL SHOULD DO WITH THIS, if anything
----------------------------------------------
1. ADOPT IT FOR THE HAPLOID AND WHOLE-GENOME CASE, which is a real gap and a clean result. A cell
   whose heterozygosity on opposite-homozygote markers, or on all called markers, sits at half the
   level the array's own quality implies, while every chromosome reads flat, is a candidate haploid
   and the tool today cannot see it at all. Separation 1.00 against bulk DNA, 0.98 against single
   diploid cells, on 12 published pronuclei. This is the recommendation worth acting on.
2. DO NOT MAKE HETEROZYGOSITY A COPY AXIS. It is not monotone in copy number: nought copies reads
   MORE heterozygous than two. Any rule of the form "less heterozygosity means fewer copies" will
   call a nullisomy normal. The call rate must be read first and must gate everything else, both per
   bin and per array.
3. WHERE IT EARNS ITS PLACE AS A SECOND OPINION: one copy against two, with both parents loaded,
   where heterozygosity on opposite homozygotes beats the intensity 0.95 to 0.71; and one copy
   against two with no parent, where the pair beats both parts, 0.86 against 0.70 and 0.77. Nowhere
   else. Two against three, and three against one, belong to the intensity.
4. ANY SUCH READING NEEDS THE CELL'S OWN BACKGROUND, measured from its normal chromosomes, and has
   to report that it degrades: drop-out runs 0.10 to 0.57 across these cells, and in the worst two
   the heterozygosity reading alone falls to 0.65 and 0.85. A cell whose background is above about
   0.45 should have the claim withheld or weakened, not silently scored.
5. NOT MEASURED HERE, and needed before any of this is built: segmental losses, because no open
   truth table carries their coordinates; and a two-copy region with published loss of
   heterozygosity, because the open set has three such rows and all three are tangled with other
   events on the same chromosome. Both gaps are in the answer key, not in the method.
